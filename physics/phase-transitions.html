Phase transitions

A phase transition is a collective change in the stable macroscopic description of matter or fields. The useful questions are not only what changed, but which variable distinguishes the phases, which symmetry or topology changed, whether a barrier had to be crossed, and what evidence makes the classification real.

Four ideas keep the subject precise.

In an infinitely large equilibrium system, a true transition appears as a non-analytic change in free energy. Real samples are finite, so singularities are rounded; experiments infer the transition from scaling, coexistence, response peaks, domain structure, and reproducible limits.

min F or min G

Free energy

At fixed temperature and volume, equilibrium minimizes Helmholtz free energy F; at fixed temperature and pressure it minimizes Gibbs free energy G. A Landau landscape is a coarse-grained function, not a literal molecular potential.

m, ρ, ψ, n̂

Order parameter

Magnetization, density difference, condensate phase, or molecular alignment can distinguish phases. Not every transition has a simple local order parameter: BKT and topological transitions are the important warning.

ξ → large

Criticality

Near a continuous transition, fluctuations correlate across growing distances. Power laws and critical exponents reveal universality: very different materials can share the same large-scale behavior.

First-order transitions cross a barrier; continuous transitions reshape it.

The same visual language separates nucleation, spinodal decomposition, and continuous ordering. Hysteresis often appears because a metastable phase survives while a barrier remains, but hysteresis is kinetic and is not itself the definition of first order.

Choose the mechanism

Continuous mode splits one minimum smoothly. First-order mode creates two competing minima and a nucleation barrier. Spinodal mode removes the barrier so fluctuations grow everywhere. Cosmic mode labels cooling events by evidential status instead of pretending the universe was a laboratory sample.

Established framework

First-order

A first derivative of free energy jumps in the thermodynamic limit. Latent heat, phase coexistence, metastability, and nucleation are characteristic. Inside the unstable spinodal region, no critical nucleus is needed.

Established framework

Continuous and critical

No latent heat is required. The order parameter changes continuously while susceptibility, heat capacity, and correlation length can diverge or follow sharp scaling. Ehrenfest's derivative classification was historically useful but does not fit every modern transition.

Matter has more ways to reorganize than solid, liquid, and gas.

The most useful taxonomy records what orders, what drives the change, and what caveat prevents a familiar label from becoming misleading.

Fluid phases

ρ, V, latent heat

Melting, boiling, and sublimation are commonly first-order. The liquid-gas coexistence line ends at a critical point; beyond it, a supercritical fluid has no sharp liquid-gas boundary.

Magnets & liquid crystals

M, orientational order

The Curie transition is the canonical symmetry-breaking example. Isotropic-to-nematic liquid-crystal transitions order molecular directions and are often weakly first-order.

Quantum collective phases

ψ, phase coherence

Superconductors expel magnetic flux and conduct without resistance; superfluids flow without ordinary viscosity; Bose-Einstein condensation and superfluidity are related but not identical.

Topology & zero-temperature change

vortices, topology, ground state

BKT transitions unbind vortex pairs without a simple local order parameter. Quantum phase transitions occur at T = 0 as pressure, field, or coupling changes the ground state; finite-temperature behavior is usually a crossover fan.

Classification remains subtle

A glass transition is not simple equilibrium freezing.

As a liquid is cooled, its relaxation time can outgrow the experiment and motion becomes arrested. The result is reproducible and dramatic, but whether and how it corresponds to an underlying equilibrium transition remains an active question.

Chemistry and biology inherit phase physics—but finite, active systems need caveats.

Molecular interactions, solvent, salt, sequence, and energy-consuming cellular machinery all reshape a free-energy landscape. Equilibrium phase language remains powerful, provided it is not applied more literally than the system allows.

DNA melting

Cooperative helix-coil transition

Heating separates the double helix into strands. The melting curve depends strongly on sequence, length, salt, and concentration rather than one universal temperature.

Lipid membranes

Gel · liquid-ordered · liquid-disordered

Model membranes show genuine coexistence among phases with different packing and mobility. Living membranes are compositionally controlled, curved, and active, so a clean equilibrium diagram is an approximation.

Protein folding

Finite free-energy landscape

Folding can be sharply cooperative, but one protein is a finite system: it has no literal bulk singularity. Multiple pathways, intermediates, solvent, and chaperones reshape the landscape.

Biomolecular condensates

Liquid-liquid phase separation

Proteins and RNA can demix into dense liquid-like compartments without membranes. In cells, reactions, transport, and active turnover mean equilibrium liquid-liquid phase separation is a starting model, not the whole story.

A quantum-field vacuum is a state, not empty nothingness.

A vacuum is a lowest-energy field configuration under specified conditions. A false vacuum is only a local minimum; quantum tunneling can, in theory, nucleate bubbles of a lower-energy state. The mechanism is precise, but its historical role in our universe is not automatically established.

Standard quantum field theory

Metastability and tunneling

A barrier can make a local minimum long-lived. A first-order transition proceeds when a critical bubble gains more volume energy than it costs in surface energy, then expands.

Cosmological application is model-dependent

False-vacuum decay

Vacuum decay is a serious theoretical possibility, not an observed event. Standard-Model parameter fits may place the Higgs vacuum in a metastable region only after extrapolating far beyond measured energies.

The early universe cooled through transitions, crossovers, and freeze-outs—not one continuous transformation.

Temperatures are better constrained than the corresponding times. Every rung below states whether it is measured indirectly through standard theory, reconstructed with lattice calculations, or still hypothetical.

  1. Inflation and reheatingbefore the hot Big Bang · timing model-dependent
    Model-dependent
    CMB observations support an early source of nearly scale-invariant perturbations, but the inflaton, reheating mechanism, and detailed energy scale remain unknown.
  2. Possible GUT transition~1015–1016 GeV · ~10-36–10-32 s
    Hypothetical
    Grand-unified symmetry breaking is theory-dependent and has not been experimentally confirmed.
  3. Electroweak crossover159.5 ± 1.5 GeV · order 10-11 s
    Standard Model + lattice
    With the measured Higgs mass, the Standard Model predicts a smooth crossover—not a first-order electroweak phase transition. Some beyond-Standard-Model theories change that result.
  4. QCD crossover~155 MeV · order 10-5 s
    Lattice-QCD supported
    At near-zero baryon chemical potential and physical quark masses, quark-gluon matter changes smoothly into hadron-dominated matter. A critical point at higher density remains unconfirmed.
  5. Neutrino decoupling and e⁺e⁻ annihilation~1–0.5 MeV · around 1 s
    Freeze-out, not a phase transition
    Interaction rates fall below expansion, then electron-positron annihilation transfers entropy mainly to photons. These are crucial thermal-history events but not symmetry-breaking phase transitions.
  6. Recombination~3000 K · ~380,000 years
    Plasma-to-neutral transition; not strict equilibrium PT
    Electrons bind to nuclei and photons begin to travel freely. It changes the observable universe, but it is not usually classified like boiling or Curie ordering.

Phase language is a bridge across sciences.

The same constrained vocabulary links engines, magnets, quantum matter, chemical mixtures, membranes, field theory, and cosmic cooling without pretending their evidence is identical.
