These diagrams preserve the angles and causal relationships that matter while compressing enormous distances and sizes.

23.44°

Tilt—not distance—drives the seasons

Earth's axis points nearly toward the same direction in space through one orbit. A hemisphere tilted sunward receives higher-angle sunlight and longer days. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, during Northern Hemisphere winter.

5.15°

New and full Moon are usually not eclipses

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5.15° to Earth's orbital plane. An eclipse requires both the right lunar phase and passage near a node where the two orbital planes cross. The 29.53-day synodic month tracks phases; the 27.32-day sidereal month tracks one orbit against the stars.

0.5°

Total or annular depends on apparent size

The Sun and Moon each span roughly half a degree in our sky. Their exact angular sizes change with distance. If the Moon looks larger, its umbra can reach Earth and totality is possible; if it looks smaller, the antumbra produces an annular eclipse. Rare hybrid eclipses switch between annular and total along their path.

Historical measurement context

Why totality opened a window onto curved spacetime

The Sun's gravity deflects light from background stars by about 1.75 arcseconds for a ray grazing the solar limb. Normally the bright photosphere hides those stars. During totality the Moon blocks the photosphere, allowing the stars' apparent positions near the Sun to be compared with reference photographs. The 1919 result was historically important but uncertain; modern radio, spacecraft, and lensing measurements are far more precise.

Eye safety: certified solar viewing protection is required throughout every partial or annular eclipse. Only observers inside the path of totality may look without a filter, and only during the brief total phase.
