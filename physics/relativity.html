Relativity

Special relativity fixes the same vacuum light speed for every inertial observer, so space and time must transform together. General relativity adds gravity by making free-fall motion follow spacetime geometry. The goal is not to make everything vague; it is to identify what every observer can still agree on.

Relativity changes coordinates, but it protects invariants.

Observers disagree about coordinates unless they first specify a frame. They can still agree on invariant intervals, on the proper time accumulated by one clock along one worldline, and on the local rule that freely falling motion removes felt weight even when spacetime is globally curved.

c = invariant

Vacuum light speed

The same vacuum light speed c appears in every inertial frame. That forces time and distance to transform together rather than independently.

Δτ = clock time

Proper time

A clock measures the time along its own worldline. Two clocks that separate and reunite can accumulate different elapsed times because they follow different spacetime paths.

free fall = local inertial frame

Curvature, not a force field on a fixed stage

Locally, free fall removes the felt weight of gravity. Globally, tidal stretching and squeezing reveal that spacetime itself is curved.

Interval classes

Lightlike paths stay on the 45° cone in units where c = 1; clocks trace timelike paths inside it. This is why causality is organized by light cones.

One reunion, two clock histories

The end events can match while the proper times differ. The straightest timelike path between nearby reunion events accumulates the most proper time.

Local free fall, global tides

Inside a small falling lab, gravity can disappear locally. Over larger separations, neighboring geodesics converge or diverge, revealing curvature.

Microscopic does not mean relativistic; the controlling ratio is energy to rest energy.

Relativistic mechanics becomes important when the kinetic energy K is no longer tiny compared with mc², or equivalently when β approaches 1. Electrons reach that regime much sooner than protons because their rest energy is much smaller. Size by itself is not the criterion.

Relativistic jets turn timing geometry into beaming and apparent superluminal motion.

Jets from active galactic nuclei, X-ray binaries, and gamma-ray bursts are powered by accretion and magnetic fields near compact objects, but the relativity lesson is geometric. If plasma moves toward us at large β and small viewing angle θ, successive photons start closer to the observer than the previous ones, compressing the arrival interval. The projected sky motion can then look faster than c even though every local signal still travels at or below c.

Why no causality is broken

The projected sky speed can exceed c because the second photon starts closer to the observer, shortening the arrival gap. No material chunk outruns its own emitted light locally.

Quantum statistics and relativity meet inside compact objects.

Packing fermions into a smaller region raises the Fermi momentum because fewer momentum states fit below a given cutoff. In white dwarfs that drives the electron gas into the relativistic regime; in neutron stars the pressure support depends on the nuclear equation of state and full general-relativistic stellar structure. This page gives the bridge, not the full mass-limit derivation.

Confinement raises momentum scale

Smaller position space means a broader momentum distribution. Degeneracy pressure is therefore tied to the available phase-space volume, not to particles literally colliding like hard beads.

White dwarfs

Electron degeneracy supports the star. As density rises, the electron Fermi momentum becomes relativistic, softening the equation of state and leading to the Chandrasekhar mass scale.

Neutron stars

Neutron degeneracy matters, but the pressure also depends on strong-interaction physics. General relativity changes hydrostatic balance, so one needs a nuclear EOS plus the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equations.

Scope note

The exact mass limits depend on composition, temperature, rotation, and the chosen EOS. This relativity page stays qualitative and points to the astrophysics chapter for the full derivations.

Relativity survives because nature can measure it.

The 1919 eclipse results mattered historically because they tested light deflection in the sky, but they were only the start. Atomic clocks, gravitational lensing, and gravitational-wave interferometers all turn relativity into quantitative measurement rather than philosophy.

Clock transport and GPS

Special- and general-relativistic clock offsets are not optional engineering corrections; navigational systems must model them to stay accurate.

Light deflection and lensing

Modern light-deflection tests use radio interferometry, spacecraft tracking, and resolved lensing systems rather than only eclipse photography.

Gravitational waves

Interferometers measure changing arm lengths caused by passing spacetime strain, turning another once-exotic GR prediction into a repeated observation.

Relativity is exact within scope, but it is not the last word on every regime.

At low speeds and in weak, slowly varying fields, relativistic equations reduce to Newtonian ones. At the other extreme, we still lack a fully tested quantum theory of gravity. And neither relativity nor quantum entanglement lets us send controlled messages outside the light cone.

The Newtonian limit

When β is small and gravitational potentials are weak, Newtonian mechanics and gravity give extraordinarily accurate approximations to the same underlying relativistic physics.

Quantum gravity remains open

General relativity treats spacetime as a classical geometry. That description is expected to fail in sufficiently extreme quantum regimes, but no experimentally confirmed complete theory has replaced it.
