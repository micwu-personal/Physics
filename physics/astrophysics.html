Astrophysics

Stars, disks, and remnants are not an arbitrary catalogue. A small number of competing effects, gravity pulling in, pressure pushing out, angular momentum resisting collapse, and collisions draining energy, decide what shapes matter can take and how each object must end.

A cloud can shed energy. It cannot shed rotation.

This asymmetry is the whole explanation. Radiation carries energy away freely, so a collapsing cloud cools and contracts. Angular momentum has no comparable escape route, so the spin survives. What follows is forced.

  1. 1

    Any cloud has some net rotation

    Turbulence and tidal torques from neighbouring matter leave every real cloud with a small, non-zero total angular momentum. Exact zero is possible in principle and essentially never realised.

  2. 2

    Contraction amplifies that rotation

    Angular momentum L = mvr is conserved, so halving the radius doubles the orbital speed. A barely turning cloud becomes a rapidly spinning one purely by shrinking.

  3. 3

    Only the axial direction is unsupported

    In the rotation plane, the centrifugal effect of orbital motion balances gravity at some radius. Parallel to the spin axis there is no such support, so material falls freely towards the midplane.

  4. 4

    Collisions destroy vertical motion, not orbits

    Particles crossing the midplane from above and below collide head-on and cancel their vertical velocities. Their shared orbital motion survives because it points the same way for everyone. Each collision thins the system.

  5. 5

    A disk is the lowest-energy shape at fixed rotation

    Once flat, further collisions become rare because neighbours move together on nearly identical orbits. The system has reached the minimum energy still allowed by the angular momentum it must keep.

The same argument, across twelve orders of magnitude.

Each system below is flat for the same reason, but the dissipation mechanism and the resulting thickness differ. Thickness is set by how much vertical motion survives, measured against orbital speed.

The Solar System

≈ 1013 m · planets within about 7° of the ecliptic

Planets formed inside a gas and dust disk around the young Sun, so they inherited its plane. Dust grains collided and stuck, and every collision averaged out vertical motion before planets existed at all.

Not perfectly flat: Mercury is inclined about 7°, and comets from the spherical Oort cloud arrive from every direction because they were scattered outward before the disk could organise them.

Saturn's rings

≈ 108 m wide, roughly 10 m thick

The most extreme flatness known. Ice particles orbit inside the Roche zone, where tides prevent them from merging into a moon, so they keep colliding gently. Each soft collision removes vertical wobble, and nothing replaces it.

The width-to-thickness ratio is about ten million to one, comparable to a sheet of paper the size of a city. Moonlets and resonances stir up local waves and vertical ripples.

The Milky Way's disk

≈ 1021 m across, about 1/100 as thick

Gas clouds collide and radiate, so the gas settles into a thin plane and forms stars there. Stars inherit the plane at birth. Once formed, stars almost never collide, so they cannot flatten further.

This is why the halo and globular clusters stay spherical: they formed before the gas settled, and collisionless stars keep the orbits they were given. The dark-matter halo is round for the same reason.

Accretion disks

104–1013 m · around black holes, neutron stars, protostars

Here dissipation is violent. Neighbouring rings orbit at different speeds, so friction and magnetic turbulence heat the gas until it radiates fiercely. That radiated energy is what makes quasars visible across the observable universe.

Thickness tracks temperature: efficient radiators stay thin, while disks that cannot cool fast enough puff up into thick tori. Losing angular momentum outward is what lets matter spiral inward at all.

Give me a star's birth mass and I can tell you how it dies.

Mass sets the central pressure, the central pressure sets the temperature, and the temperature decides which nuclear reactions can run. Everything else, lifetime, colour, size, final remnant, and the elements returned to space, follows from that chain.

Chandrasekhar limit ≈ 1.4 M☉

The maximum mass supportable by electron degeneracy pressure.

  1. The Pauli exclusion principle forbids two electrons from sharing a quantum state. Compressing the gas forces electrons into higher-momentum states, producing a pressure that does not vanish even at zero temperature.
  2. For slow electrons this gives P ∝ ρ^(5/3). Balancing it against gravity yields R ∝ M^(−1/3): adding mass makes the star smaller, so density climbs quickly.
  3. Once electrons approach the speed of light their momentum can no longer grow without bound, and the relation softens to P ∝ ρ^(4/3).
  4. With that exponent, the outward support energy and the inward gravitational energy both scale as 1/R. Radius cancels completely, so the balance no longer selects a size; it selects a single mass.
  5. That mass is M ≈ 5.83/μₑ² M☉. For carbon-oxygen matter with two nucleons per electron, μₑ = 2 and M ≈ 1.44 M☉.

Above it, no radius satisfies equilibrium and collapse cannot be halted by electrons. This is why Type Ia supernovae are so uniform, and why every white dwarf ever weighed sits below the line.

Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit ≈ 2.2 M☉

The maximum mass of a neutron star, and the least certain number here.

  1. When electron degeneracy fails, electrons are forced into protons: p + e⁻ → n + ν. The core becomes almost pure neutrons, and neutron degeneracy pressure takes over.
  2. Neutrons are about 1839 times heavier than electrons, so they stay non-relativistic to far higher densities. That buys real additional support.
  3. Newtonian gravity is no longer adequate. In general relativity pressure itself carries energy and therefore gravitates, so adding pressure adds weight. The Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equation replaces hydrostatic equilibrium.
  4. Oppenheimer and Volkoff solved this in 1939 for a gas of free neutrons and obtained only about 0.7 M☉, below stars already known. The missing ingredient was the strong repulsion between nucleons at short range.
  5. Including nuclear forces raises the ceiling to roughly 2.2–2.3 M☉. Pulsars near 2 M☉ set a firm floor, and the merger GW170817 constrains it from above.

Unlike the Chandrasekhar mass, this value is not derived from first principles alone. It depends on the equation of state of matter denser than an atomic nucleus, which remains an open research question.

Eddington luminosity

The brightest a spherical object can shine before its own light blows it apart.

  1. Outgoing photons scatter off free electrons and push them outward. The force on one electron is L σ_T / (4π r² c), where σ_T is the Thomson cross-section.
  2. Electrons cannot leave alone; electrostatic attraction drags protons with them. So gravity must be evaluated on the pair, essentially one proton mass: G M m_p / r².
  3. Both forces fall off as 1/r², so distance cancels. The balance holds everywhere at once or nowhere at all.
  4. Setting them equal gives L_Edd = 4π G M m_p c / σ_T ≈ 1.26 × 10³¹ (M/M☉) watts, about 32 000 solar luminosities per solar mass.

This caps how fast a black hole can grow by spherical accretion and explains why the most massive stars shed matter in fierce winds. Real sources exceed it when geometry is not spherical, since radiation can escape through thin channels while matter falls in elsewhere.

A standard candle is a source whose true brightness you can know.

Apparent brightness alone cannot give distance, because a faint nearby object and a brilliant distant one look identical. A standard candle breaks the tie: some locally measurable property, unaffected by distance, reveals the intrinsic luminosity.

A rising line relating pulsation period to luminosity, with a marker travelling along it.

Why a period fixes a luminosity

A pulsating star behaves like a resonating sphere. Its period depends on its mean density, and density connects to mass, which connects to luminosity. Timing the pulse therefore weighs the star.

Cepheid variables

Useful to about 30 Mpc

A layer of partly ionised helium acts as a valve. Compression makes it more opaque, trapping radiation and building pressure until the star expands; expansion lets helium recombine, opacity drops, the trapped heat escapes and the star falls back. This kappa mechanism sets a clock.

Standard because the period reflects mean density, and more luminous Cepheids are larger and less dense, so they pulse more slowly. Dust reddening and metallicity must still be corrected for.

Type Ia supernovae

Useful beyond 1000 Mpc

A white dwarf accreting from a companion detonates when it nears the Chandrasekhar mass. Because that trigger mass is nearly the same everywhere, the amount of nickel-56 produced is nearly the same, and radioactive nickel decay powers the light curve.

Not truly identical. They are standardised using the Phillips relation: intrinsically brighter events fade more slowly, so light-curve shape corrects the peak brightness. This is the tool that revealed accelerating expansion.

Tip of the red giant branch

Useful to about 20 Mpc

A low-mass star climbing the red giant branch ignites helium the instant its degenerate core reaches a critical mass, which is almost independent of the star's total mass or composition.

That fixed core mass produces a sharp, repeatable cutoff in brightness at the top of the branch. It needs no repeat observations, unlike Cepheids, and provides an independent cross-check on the distance ladder.

Why the ladder needs rungs

Parallax → Cepheids → Type Ia

No single method spans all distances. Geometric parallax calibrates nearby Cepheids; Cepheids in galaxies that also hosted a Type Ia calibrate those supernovae; the supernovae then reach cosmological distances.

Every rung inherits the errors of the one below. The persistent disagreement between ladder-based and early-universe measurements of the expansion rate is an unresolved problem, not a settled result.
